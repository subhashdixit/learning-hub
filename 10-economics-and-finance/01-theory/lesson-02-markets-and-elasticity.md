## The idea

**Analogy:** A demand curve holds other conditions still; a real-world scatterplot often mixes several moving curves.

Demand relates quantity demanded to price under stated other conditions; supply relates quantity supplied to price. An equilibrium is where their planned quantities agree within the model. A shift in income/preferences/costs differs from movement along a fixed curve. Shortage/surplus reasoning depends on institutional and adjustment assumptions.

Price elasticity is percentage quantity response divided by percentage price change under a specified comparison; midpoint formulas reduce base-direction asymmetry for finite changes. A demand elasticity estimate needs a causal identification strategy if interpreted as response to changing price. Simultaneous supply changes, seasonality and promotions confound naive correlations.

Competition, monopoly, differentiated products and strategic interaction create different pricing/incentive structures. Consumer/producer surplus and externalities help analyse welfare under assumptions, but a diagram is not a substitute for institutional evidence. Business analysts should state which causal or descriptive claim the data can support.

## Work through it

A hypothetical price rise 10% causes quantity to fall 15% under an isolated demand response: elasticity≈−1.5. Revenue multiplies by 1.10×.85=.935, a 6.5% fall. Finite-change revenue cannot be computed by simply adding 10−15=−5% without the product term.

## Common mistakes and practical limits

Do not estimate demand elasticity from a raw price/quantity correlation and call it causal. A forecast at a new price can fail if the observed history did not contain comparable interventions.
